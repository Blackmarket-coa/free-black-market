## 1. Product thesis

Free Black Market (FBM) should be a **painkiller, not a vitamin**. It must solve immediate, recurring inefficiencies in how people buy, sell, source, ship, finance, and coordinate commerce. A user should recognize a problem with their current way of doing business and find a practical, compelling solution through FBM.

**Core promise:** FBM makes it easier to buy better, sell independently, save money, build businesses, and participate in a community-driven economy—without sacrificing convenience, price, speed, or usability.

The goal is not simply to move transactions from one marketplace to another. FBM should make independent economic participation practical, accessible, and rewarding.

This document defines product direction. It does not assert that every described capability is implemented or production-ready. Use code, tests, operational evidence, and the relevant capability documentation to establish current status.

## 2. The users and the jobs FBM must do

### Buyers: shop with intelligence

Buyers need to understand what they are buying, who they are buying from, what the purchase really costs, and what meaningful alternatives exist.

FBM should help buyers:

- Understand product origin, maker/manufacturer, materials or ingredients, provenance, product attributes, and available verification.
- Assess seller trust through transparent histories, reviews, fulfillment records, certifications, and product information.
- See seller values, affiliations, and stated political or community alignment through voluntary, clearly sourced disclosures and user-selected filters. Never infer political beliefs or affiliations.
- Understand where money goes: seller proceeds, platform fees, payment processing, shipping, taxes, and any explicitly disclosed community or impact allocation.
- Compare the **full delivered price**, not just the listing price.
- Find low-cost options, faster delivery, local alternatives, and lower-impact options while seeing tradeoffs and ranking criteria.
- Discover healthier or lower-hazard alternatives using substantiated ingredient/material information.
- Share useful product links and coordinate with other buyers for bulk purchases or shared shipping.

### Sellers and organizations: build with independence

FBM should provide the capabilities of an integrated business operating system without forcing sellers to become corporate or surrender ownership, customer relationships, or control.

Help independent businesses, producers, nonprofits, and community organizations:

- Start selling through smooth onboarding and guided setup.
- Operate through modern, approachable tools for products, inventory, orders, customers, fulfillment, and business performance.
- Access customers, creators, collaborators, suppliers, and trade partners.
- Market products through practical plans, campaign tools, competitor intelligence, and creator discovery.
- Sell across channels without losing a coherent view of their business.
- Find B2B sourcing, procurement, wholesale, barter, and trade opportunities.
- Discover appropriate financing and capital-access pathways.
- Learn business strategies in the context of the work they are doing.
- Use workflows designed for nonprofits and community organizations, not only conventional for-profit businesses.
- Keep platform costs and commissions low, predictable, and transparent.

## 3. The problems FBM should remove

### Buyer-side friction

- Opaque money flows and unclear seller incentives.
- Listing prices that hide shipping, taxes, fees, and delivery tradeoffs.
- Cheap goods that become expensive or slow after distant shipping.
- Difficulty finding independent producers, local suppliers, and manufacturers.
- Poor access to trustworthy product information and substantiated health-related attributes.
- Difficulty comparing lower-impact options without vague or misleading claims.
- Lack of practical ways to coordinate bulk purchases and shared demand.

### Seller-side friction

- High commissions, subscriptions, and fragmented business software.
- Difficult onboarding and too many disconnected tools to start or scale.
- Weak customer access, limited marketing support, and difficulty building social proof.
- Expensive shipping and limited logistics options.
- Platform lock-in and dependence on corporate infrastructure.
- B2B tools that process transactions but do little to create durable relationships.
- Limited access to trade, alternative financing, creators, and collaborators.
- Conventional commerce software that poorly serves nonprofits and community groups.

People currently cope by accepting opaque platforms, paying more for distant shipping, delaying or abandoning business ideas, or choosing employment because independent business is too complicated. FBM should make better alternatives easier to act on.

## 4. Core product capabilities

### Buyer intelligence

1. **Competitive Price Index:** compare genuinely comparable products and sellers.
2. **Delivered-cost comparison:** item price, shipping, taxes, fees, delivery estimate, and total.
3. **Decision support:** lowest-cost, fastest-delivery, local, and lower-impact alternatives, with transparent ranking logic.
4. **Product transparency:** provenance, ingredients/materials, certifications, seller verification, and trust signals.
5. **Money-flow transparency:** show how a purchase is divided, using the actual fee and payout rules.
6. **Group purchasing:** buyer-to-buyer coordination, group buys, bulk-buy prompts, and shared shipping opportunities.
7. **Useful discovery:** find local products, producers, suppliers, and alternatives.

### Seller and organization enablement

1. Guided business launch and low-friction onboarding.
2. A coherent operating hub for products, inventory, orders, customers, fulfillment, and performance.
3. Competitive pricing and market intelligence.
4. Marketing plans, campaign assistance, competitor monitoring, and creator discovery.
5. B2B discovery, sourcing, procurement, trade matching, and relationship management.
6. Financing discovery and appropriate capital-access pathways.
7. Multichannel selling and portable customer relationships.
8. Nonprofit and community-organization workflows.
9. Business education and strategies embedded in operational workflows.

## 5. Recurring utility and habit loops

FBM should earn frequent use through recurring utility, not artificial engagement mechanics. The target is the practical frequency of everyday commerce and gig-economy tools such as Amazon, Shopify, Uber Eats, Faire, and Etsy.

1. **Check the market.** Return for orders, shipments, inventory, price changes, supplier updates, business performance, and new opportunities.
2. **Compare before buying.** Make FBM a natural first stop to compare delivered prices, speed, local options, product information, and alternatives.
3. **Share useful finds.** Make product links easy to share with friends, customers, groups, and organizations. Sharing should help recipients make better decisions and may create measurable value for the sharer.
4. **Hunt, gather, and discover.** Make finding products, producers, suppliers, resources, and trade opportunities feel like modern foraging: discovery that leads to useful real-world action.
5. **Build and improve a business.** Give sellers reasons to return for orders, customer activity, insights, marketing opportunities, pricing recommendations, suppliers, and trade/financing leads.

Connect commerce with well-being through tangible feedback: money saved, progress made, successful fulfillment, local connections, useful discoveries, and community participation.

## 6. Product principles

1. **Affirm good decisions.** Recognize measurable benefits; do not use empty praise or manipulative flattery.
2. **Show the full picture.** Disclose costs, money flows, product information, and ranking criteria.
3. **Make better options convenient.** Compare price, quality, speed, health-related attributes, locality, and impact without assuming one dimension always wins.
4. **Reduce complexity.** Make business ownership accessible to people without business or technical expertise.
5. **Connect rather than isolate.** Connect buyers, sellers, producers, nonprofits, creators, investors, and communities.
6. **Reward useful participation.** Prioritize economic and community value over empty engagement.
7. **Preserve independence.** Provide infrastructure without unnecessary lock-in or surrender of ownership and control.
8. **Be evidence-led.** Distinguish verified facts, seller claims, estimates, and unknowns—especially for political alignment, health, and environmental impact.
9. **Build on what exists.** Reuse working modules and shared infrastructure; avoid duplicating domains or creating platform bloat.

## 7. Trust, claims, and transparency rules

- Political values and affiliations must be voluntary disclosures with clear provenance, dates, and user control. Do not infer sensitive beliefs from behavior, purchases, demographics, or associations.
- Do not describe products as “chemical-free.” All material things consist of chemicals. Use specific ingredient/material disclosures, “no added [specified substance]” statements, recognized certifications, and substantiated claims.
- Health-related comparisons must be factual, appropriately qualified, and supported by reliable product data. Do not turn marketplace filters into medical advice.
- Environmental rankings must expose criteria, data source, uncertainty, and tradeoffs. Do not claim “least harmful” without a defined and defensible comparison.
- Distinguish seller-provided information from independently verified information.
- Never fabricate savings, impact, verification, delivery estimates, or product attributes.
- Show the actual money path and fee calculation. Under the current platform fee policy, payment-processing fees are deducted from the sale before the seller share is calculated; the 3% Coalition fee covers basic platform costs. Do not imply that the Coalition fee is an additional fee on top of the agreed calculation. Keep public fee explanations synchronized with the implemented payout breakdown and [docs/ADDON_COMMITMENTS.md](ADDON_COMMITMENTS.md).

## 8. Measurement: outcomes over registrations

Measure repeat utility and user outcomes, not registrations alone.

### Buyer measures

- Buyer activation and repeat purchase.
- Comparisons per active buyer and conversion from comparison to purchase.
- Verified buyer savings and delivered-cost differences.
- Delivery speed and fulfillment reliability.
- Product-information coverage and trust outcomes.
- Shared product links, bulk-buy participation, and group-buy savings.
- Local sourcing and repeat buyer–seller relationships.

### Seller and organization measures

- Seller activation and retention.
- Time to first live listing and time to first sale.
- Repeat revenue, order completion, and fulfillment reliability.
- Platform fees and total operating cost.
- Customer access and marketing outcomes.
- Trade matches and completed exchanges.
- Nonprofit/community organization activation and continued use.

### Platform measures

- Weekly and daily repeat visits tied to useful tasks.
- Repeat utility across buyer and seller workflows.
- Successful discovery-to-transaction journeys.
- Measured savings and community benefit.
- Environmental and health-related indicators only where defensible data exists.

## 9. Implementation sequence

Prioritize the first set of painkillers before attempting the entire economic ecosystem. Sequence is a product priority, not a statement of current implementation status.

### First wave: immediate, repeatable value

1. Full delivered-cost breakdown and transparent price comparison.
2. Product and seller transparency, trust information, and clear claim provenance.
3. Local discovery and shipping comparisons.
4. Smooth seller onboarding and a simple operating dashboard.
5. Product-link sharing and basic buyer-to-buyer coordination.
6. Useful buyer and seller activity summaries.

### Next wave: deeper market intelligence and coordination

- Competitive Price Index at meaningful scale.
- Bulk/group purchasing workflows and shared shipping.
- Better product attribute data and evidence-backed healthier/lower-hazard alternatives.
- Competitor marketing intelligence and creator access.
- B2B sourcing, trade, and financing opportunities.
- Multichannel operations and nonprofit/community workflows.
- More complete money-flow and impact reporting.

### Later ecosystem development

Build broader community economic infrastructure—financing programs, trade networks, shared productive assets, cooperative services, and other community systems—on top of validated buyer and seller utility. Keep regulated or money-moving capabilities behind their existing legal, compliance, and operational gates.
